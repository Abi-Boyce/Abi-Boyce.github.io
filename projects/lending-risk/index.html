Lending analysis · LendingClub public loan data, 2007–2011 · SQL (DuckDB) and Python (scikit-learn) · by Abigayle Boyce

A simple model on application data cut bad loans at the same approval rate, and over half its declines came down to loan term

I wanted to practice the core question in consumer lending: who do you approve, and what does it cost in losses? I loaded 39,747 finished LendingClub loans into DuckDB, explored them with SQL, then trained a default model in Python on 2007–2010 loans and tested it on 2011 loans, the way it would be used on new applicants.

1. Risk climbs with grade, and faster on longer loans

5. Fair lending and adverse action

When a lender declines someone, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B require giving them the specific main reasons (or telling them how to ask). I built that into the analysis instead of treating it as an afterthought:

  • Left out location. State and zip code can stand in for race or ethnicity, so the model doesn't use them.
  • Reasons people can act on. For each applicant the model would decline at an 80% approval rate, I found the factor that raised their risk the most. Grade is excluded from reasons because it's a summary score, not a reason.

Top decline reason, declined 2011 applicants

6. A counter-offer instead of a decline?

Why this needs a test, not a launch: the term effect here is a correlation. People who choose 60 months may differ in ways the data doesn't show, and a 36-month term on the same amount means a higher monthly payment. The right next step is an experiment that offers shorter terms to a small group and measures both take-up and losses.

This matters for products where the borrower picks a payment plan at checkout: the plan you show can change risk as much as the yes-or-no decision.

How I built it, and how I checked it

  1. Load and clean in SQL. Typed every column, kept only finished loans, and left out 2,749 loans issued under an older credit policy.
  2. Explore in SQL by grade, term, purpose, inquiries and issue year.
  3. Model in Python. Logistic regression on 15 application-time features, trained on 2007–2010 and tested on 2011.
  4. Guard against leakage. Payment and recovery columns describe what happened after the loan, so they're excluded.
  5. Use AI, then verify. I wrote this with an AI assistant (Claude) and re-ran every result. I spot-checked numbers with separate queries, for example the 2011 bad rate (15.2% of all 2011 loans; 15.0% after dropping rows with missing fields) and the 9,650 grade A, 36-month loans.

Code

01_load.sql · 02_analysis.sql · 03_model.py · 04_export.py · README

-- Bad rate by recent credit inquiries, within the same grade
SELECT grade,
       CASE WHEN inq_last_6mths >= 3 THEN '3+'
            ELSE CAST(CAST(inq_last_6mths AS INT) AS VARCHAR) END AS inquiries,
       count(*) AS loans,
       round(100*avg(bad),1) AS bad_rate_pct
FROM loans
WHERE grade IN ('A','B','C','D')
GROUP BY 1,2 ORDER BY 1,2;

Limits

  • One lender, one era (2007–2011), before today's buy-now-pay-later products. Patterns can differ now.
  • Only approved loans are in the data, so it can't show how declined applicants would have performed.
  • Logistic regression is a baseline. Gradient-boosted models usually rank better but need more care to explain.
